42  Glossary

One line each, in alphabetical order, with a link to the unit that does the explaining. Where two terms are routinely confused they are defined next to each other on purpose.

Alternative hypothesis. What counts as evidence against the null; it fixes which results are “extreme”. S9

Base rate. How common something is before you look at any evidence. Without it a conditional probability cannot be reversed. S4

Bootstrap. Estimating uncertainty by resampling your own data with replacement, thousands of times. S8

Broadcasting. NumPy’s rule for combining arrays of different shapes by stretching a dimension of size 1. P7

Central limit theorem. The sample mean becomes approximately normal as \(n\) grows, whatever the population looks like, provided the variance is finite. It says nothing about the individual observations. S7

Chain rule. The derivative of \(f(g(x))\) is \(f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)\); the second factor is the one people forget. M4

Condition number. How much a system of equations amplifies small errors in its inputs. M10

Confidence interval. A range built by a procedure that captures the true value a stated fraction of the time across repeated studies. The percentage belongs to the procedure, not to the interval in front of you. S8

Confounder. A variable related to both the thing you varied and the thing you measured, whose influence gets credited to something else. S12

Correlation (\(r\)). A unitless measure of linear association between \(-1\) and \(+1\). \(r = 0\) means no linear relationship, not no relationship. S3

Covariance. The unscaled version of correlation; its sign is informative and its size depends on the units you chose. S3

Density. For a continuous variable, the function whose area gives probability. Its height is not a probability and may exceed 1. S5

Derivative. The rate at which a function responds to a small change in its input; the gradient of the tangent. M3

Design. How units got from the population into your table. It decides which conclusions are defensible. S1

Eigenvector. A direction a matrix stretches without turning; its eigenvalue is the stretch factor. M12

Estimand. The number you want, defined on the population. Distinct from the estimate, which is the number you compute from a sample. S1

Expected value. The long-run average of a random variable; a balance point, often not a possible outcome. S5

Gradient. The vector of partial derivatives; points in the direction of steepest increase. M6

Independence. \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)\): knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Not the same as mutually exclusive, which means they cannot both happen and makes them strongly dependent. S4

Integral. Accumulated area under a curve; the reverse of differentiation. M7

Interquartile range (IQR). \(Q_3 - Q_1\), the width of the middle half. S2

Least squares. Choosing coefficients to minimise the sum of squared residuals. M11

Mean. The balance point of a set of values; sensitive to extremes. Compare the median, the half-way point by count, which is not. S2

Multiple comparisons. Running many tests, so that false positives become the expected outcome rather than the exception. S12

Nominal / ordinal. Categories without an order, and categories with one but without known spacing. A median makes sense for the second and not the first. S1

Normal distribution. The bell-shaped family that arises when many small independent effects add up; 68–95–99.7 within one, two and three standard deviations. S6

Null hypothesis. The specific claim a test assumes while computing how surprising the data would be. S9

Overdispersion. Count data more variable than a Poisson allows — variance above the mean. S6

Parameter. A fixed, usually unknown, property of a population, written with a Greek letter. S1

Population. The entire group a question is about, as opposed to the sample, which is what you measured. S1

Power. The probability of detecting a real effect of a given size. Low power makes non-significant results uninformative and published findings less reliable. S9

Prediction interval. A range for one new observation; wider than a confidence interval for the mean, and it does not shrink to nothing as \(n\) grows. S11

\(p\)-value. The probability, computed assuming the null is true, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one. Not the probability that the null is true. S9

Quantile. A value at or below which approximately a stated proportion of the data lies; the median is the 0.5-quantile. S2

\(R^2\). The proportion of variance in the outcome accounted for by the fitted model. Not a measure of whether the model is correct. S11

Random variable. A rule assigning a number to each outcome of a random process. S5

Regression to the mean. Units selected for being extreme are closer to average next time, with no cause required. S12

Residual. Observed value minus fitted value; what the model did not account for. S11

Sampling distribution. The distribution of a statistic across all the samples you might have taken. S7

Simpson’s paradox. An association that reverses when a third variable is taken into account. S12

Standard deviation (\(s\), \(\sigma\)). How spread out the observations are. Does not shrink as you collect more data. S2

Standard error. How much a statistic moves between samples; for a mean, \(s/\sqrt{n}\). Shrinks as you collect more data. S7

Survivorship bias. Drawing conclusions from only the units that made it into the data. S12

Transpose. Flipping a matrix’s rows and columns; \((AB)^{\mathsf{T}} = B^{\mathsf{T}}A^{\mathsf{T}}\). M9

\(t\) distribution. The distribution used in place of the normal when the population standard deviation has been estimated from the data; slightly wider, and more so for small samples. S8

Type I / Type II error. Rejecting a true null; failing to reject a false one. Their rates are \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), and you choose only the first. S9

Unit. What one row of your table represents, and the thing that must be independently sampled for the standard formulas to apply. S1

Variance. The average squared distance from the mean; the square of the standard deviation, in squared units. S2, S5
