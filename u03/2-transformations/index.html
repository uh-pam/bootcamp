Hook · 4 min

Where does the square go?

★ essentialMP

A 2×22 \times 22×2 matrix times a vector gives a new vector, so the matrix sends every point of the plane somewhere else. Image editors rotate, flip and skew pictures this way.

Take

A=(2112)\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}A=(21​12​)

and the unit square, with corners (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0), (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0), (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) and (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1).

Predict first
Before you run anything: where does the corner (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) go? Is the new shape still a square?
Predict3b

Work out A (1,1)\mathbf{A}\,(1, 1)A(1,1) by hand. Give both components, separated by a semicolon.

One dot product per row of A\mathbf{A}A: row 1 is (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1).
Solution
(2×1+1×1,  1×1+2×1)=(3,3)(2 \times 1 + 1 \times 1,\; 1 \times 1 + 2 \times 1) = (3, 3)(2×1+1×1,1×1+2×1)=(3,3).

Now all four corners at once. Put them as the columns of one matrix, and one @ moves them all:

Python

Not a square any more: a parallelogram, stretched along the diagonal. For every matrix the origin stays put, and straight lines stay straight (or, if the matrix flattens the plane, may collapse to a point).

Worked example · 5 min

Four moves to recognise

★ essentialMP

Most 2×22 \times 22×2 matrices you meet are one of these moves, or a mix of them.

Worked example: reading a matrix from its columns
R=(0−110)\mathbf{R} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}R=(01​−10​)
  1. Column 1 is where e1\mathbf{e}_1e1​ lands: (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1). The arrow that pointed right now points up.
  2. Column 2 is where e2\mathbf{e}_2e2​ lands: (−1,0)(-1, 0)(−1,0). The arrow that pointed up now points left.
  3. Name the move. Both arrows turned a quarter-turn anticlockwise and kept their length, so the whole plane did: R\mathbf{R}R rotates by 90°.
  4. Check one point. R (2,1)=2 (0,1)+1 (−1,0)=(−1,2)\mathbf{R}\,(2, 1) = 2\,(0, 1) + 1\,(-1, 0) = (-1, 2)R(2,1)=2(0,1)+1(−1,0)=(−1,2): turned a quarter-turn, as it should be.

Use it · 5 min

Directions that only stretch

★ essentialMP

Back to A=(2112)\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}A=(21​12​) from the start. Try it on a few arrows:

Python

Most arrows are turned onto a new line: (1,0)→(2,1)(1, 0) \to (2, 1)(1,0)→(2,1). But (1,1)→(3,3)(1, 1) \to (3, 3)(1,1)→(3,3) stays on its line, three times as long, and (1,−1)(1, -1)(1,−1) does not move at all.

A vector v≠0\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}v=0 with

Av=λv\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}Av=λv

is an eigenvector of A\mathbf{A}A, and the number λ\lambdaλ (“lambda”) is its eigenvalue: the stretch factor. For this A\mathbf{A}A: (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) with λ=3\lambda = 3λ=3, and (1,−1)(1, -1)(1,−1) with λ=1\lambda = 1λ=1. Any multiple of an eigenvector, such as (−1,−1)(-1, -1)(−1,−1), is one too.

λ\lambdaλWhat happens along that direction
greater than 1stretched
between 0 and 1shrunk
1unchanged
negativeflipped to point the other way, and scaled by the size of λ\lambdaλ

To test a candidate, work out Av\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}Av and ask: is it the same number times every component of v\mathbf{v}v? For a diagonal matrix the axes always pass, with the diagonal entries as the λ\lambdaλs. The 90° rotation has no eigenvector in the plane: it turns every arrow.
