Worked example: a spam filter

20% of emails are spam. A filter flags 95% of spam and 2% of genuine emails. An email is flagged: what is P(spam∣flagged)P(\text{spam} \mid \text{flagged})P(spam∣flagged)?

  1. Natural frequencies: out of 1000 emails, 200 are spam and 800 genuine.
  2. Flagged spam: 95% of 200 = 190. Flagged genuine: 2% of 800 = 16.
  3. Flagged in all: 190 + 16 = 206, so P(spam∣flagged)=190/206≈0.922P(\text{spam} \mid \text{flagged}) = 190/206 \approx 0.922P(spam∣flagged)=190/206≈0.922.
  4. The formula gives the same: P(E)=0.95×0.20+0.02×0.80=0.206P(E) = 0.95 \times 0.20 + 0.02 \times 0.80 = 0.206P(E)=0.95×0.20+0.02×0.80=0.206, and P(H∣E)=0.19/0.206≈0.922P(H \mid E) = 0.19 / 0.206 \approx 0.922P(H∣E)=0.19/0.206≈0.922.

Use it · 5 min

The base-rate effect

★ essentialSP

Keep the test (sensitivity 0.90, false-positive rate 0.09) and change only the prior.

Python
prior P(D)P(D)P(D)0.010.100.50
posterior P(D∣+)P(D \mid +)P(D∣+)0.0920.5260.909

The curve always sits above the dashed line, since a positive raises the chance, but for small priors it stays low. This is the base-rate effect. When DDD is rare, a 9% false-positive rate hits a huge group (9 900 people) and swamps the true positives from a tiny one (100 people). At a prior of 0.5 the posterior, 0.909, even passes the sensitivity, 0.90.

Ignoring the prior is the base-rate fallacy. That is why a positive screen is followed by a second, different test: 0.092 becomes its prior (Lesson 9.3).
