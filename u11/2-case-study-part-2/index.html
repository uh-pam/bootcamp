Reading regression output

★ essentialMSP

stats.linregress(x, y) fits the same least-squares line as np.polyfit, and also reports how sure you can be of it. For the annual means against year, 1853–2025, its parts print as:

PartValueReading
res.slope0.00982°C per year: 0.098 °C per decade, 0.98 °C per century (Unit 6)
res.intercept−5.03The line’s value in year 0: an extrapolation 1,853 years before the data, so it has no meaning (Unit 6)
res.rvalue0.54Pearson’s rrr. Square it: r2=0.29r^2 = 0.29r2=0.29, so the trend accounts for 29% of the year-to-year variation; the rest is scatter around the line, mostly weather
res.pvalue1.7×10−141.7 \times 10^{-14}1.7×10−14A test of H0H_0H0​: the true slope is 0 (Unit 10). If there were no trend, a slope this steep would almost never arise: strong evidence of a trend. It says nothing about the cause
res.stderr0.00117The SE of the slope: how much the fitted slope would vary between samples

The SE gives a 95% interval for the slope, as in Lesson 10.3, with n−2n - 2n−2 degrees of freedom (the line estimates two numbers, mmm and ccc):

  1. t∗t^{*}t∗ = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 2) =1.974= 1.974=1.974 for n−2=171n - 2 = 171n−2=171.
  2. Margin: 1.974×0.00117=0.002311.974 \times 0.00117 = 0.002311.974×0.00117=0.00231 °C per year.
  3. Interval: 0.00982±0.002310.00982 \pm 0.002310.00982±0.00231, so 0.0075 to 0.0121 °C per year: 0.75 to 1.21 °C per century.

This interval, like the p-value, treats each year’s scatter around the line as independent of its neighbours’. Here that is only roughly true: warm years tend to follow warm years (neighbouring residuals have a correlation of about 0.3). So read 0.75 to 1.21 °C per century as approximate, and probably a little too narrow.

Your code11b

Reproduce the output above: store stats.linregress of the annual means against year as res and print its parts. Then store the slope in °C per century as per_century, and the ends of its 95% interval, also in °C per century, as slope_lo and slope_hi.

res = stats.linregress(x, y), with x the years and y the annual means; then print(res.slope) and so on.
tstar = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 2) with n = len(x).
slope_lo = 100 * (res.slope - tstar * res.stderr), and the same with + for slope_hi.
Go deeper (optional) · What the p-value assumes

Like the t-test, linregress’s SE and p-value treat the residuals as independent. Here neighbouring years’ residuals have a correlation of 0.31 (partly because of the curve you saw), so the true uncertainty is somewhat larger than stated. Time-series methods (Semester B) allow for this. A common rough allowance counts the 173 correlated years as about 92 independent ones, and widens the slope’s interval to about 0.66 to 1.30 °C per century. With p=1.7×10−14p = 1.7 \times 10^{-14}p=1.7×10−14, the conclusion “there is a trend” still stands.

How sure is +1.19 °C?

★ essentialSP

Goal: a 95% bootstrap percentile interval (Lesson 7.3) for the headline difference, recent mean − early mean. Treat each period’s 30 years as a sample of the years its climate could produce, each year an independent draw, as in Lesson 10.3. The end of this step asks how far that holds.

Your code11b

Select the two periods as arrays of 30 annual means, early_years (1926–1955) and recent_years (1996–2025); in part 1, early and recent were their means. Make 2000 bootstrap differences with rng = np.random.default_rng(1), resampling each period separately, and store the ends of the 95% percentile interval as lo and hi.

early_years = annual.loc[1926:1955].to_numpy() gives the 30 early years: .loc includes both ends.
In each of 2000 rounds, draw rng.choice(recent_years, size=len(recent_years), replace=True) and the same from early_years, then append the difference of the two means.
lo, hi = np.percentile(boot_diffs, [2.5, 97.5])
With seed 1 and recent_years drawn first: 0.88 to 1.49 °C. Another seed moves the ends by about 0.01 °C.

Check with Unit 10. Welch’s t-test on the same 30 + 30 years agrees:

Here t∗≈2.00t^{*} \approx 2.00t∗≈2.00: Welch’s test uses about 57 degrees of freedom, which SciPy works out for you.

Two methods, one answer: the recent 30 years were about 1.2 °C warmer, and plausibly anywhere from about 0.9 to 1.5 °C warmer.

What both intervals assume. Each treats every year as an independent draw from its period’s climate. That is only roughly true: neighbouring years are alike, and the recent period is itself still warming, by about 0.4 °C per decade within 1996–2025. So both intervals are approximate, probably a little too narrow; time-series methods (Semester B) would widen them somewhat. A gap of about 7.6 SEs leaves “warmer” in no doubt; the exact ends of the interval are less sure.

Your conclusion in 150 words

★ essentialS

Goal: answer the question for a reader who has not seen your code. Write about 150 words under Interpret in your notebook, or on paper, before you open the model answer.

Then check your draft. Does it:

  • answer the question in its first sentence, in plain words;
  • give the size of the change, with units (°C, and °C per decade or century);
  • give the uncertainty: a 95% interval, or a p-value said in words, and say that it is approximate, because neighbouring years are alike;
  • say that a straight line gives an average rate, and that the warming has been faster recently;
  • put r2r^2r2 in words: most of the year-to-year variation is weather;
  • say what the data can’t show: the cause, places other than Oxford, the future, and how changes in instruments and surroundings over 170 years may affect the record;
  • avoid jargon a general reader would not follow, or explain it?
Refresh Model answer

Yes: Oxford is warmer now than a century ago. The average daily maximum temperature was 15.25 °C over 1996–2025 and 14.06 °C over 1926–1955, a rise of 1.19 °C. A 95% interval for the rise is roughly 0.9 to 1.5 °C: approximate, and probably a little too narrow, because neighbouring years are alike. A Welch t-test agrees (p≈3×10−10p \approx 3 \times 10^{-10}p≈3×10−10): a gap this large would almost never come from year-to-year weather alone.

The warming has not been steady. A straight line through 1853–2025 rises about 1.0 °C per century (roughly 0.75 to 1.2 °C), but the residuals curve upwards at the end: since 1926 the rate is about 0.17 °C per decade. The trend accounts for about 29% of the variation between years; the rest is mostly weather.

These data come from one station. They show that Oxford warmed, not why, and changes in instruments and surroundings since 1853 may affect the record. They do not predict the future.
