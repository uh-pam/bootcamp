The question and the data

★ essentialS

Welcome back. Coursework is due soon, so this lesson is built in save points: each step stands on its own, and you can stop after any of them.

The question for this lesson and the next:

Is Oxford warmer now than it was a century ago?

The data. The Met Office has recorded the weather at its Oxford station every month since January 1853. oxford.csv holds 2076 months, to December 2025:

ColumnMeaning
year, monthwhen (month 1 = January)
tmaxmean daily maximum temperature in that month (°C)
tminmean daily minimum temperature (°C)
afdays of air frost
raintotal rainfall (mm)
sunhours of sunshine

Source: Met Office historic station data. Contains public sector information licensed under the Open Government Licence v3.0.

What does “a century ago” mean? You have to decide, and say why.

CompareForAgainst
one year with one year, e.g. 1925 with 2025simpleone year’s mean often sits half a degree or more from the next year’s, so the answer depends on which two years you pick
two 30-year periods30 years is a climate normal: the Met Office and the World Meteorological Organization describe a climate by 30-year averages, e.g. 1991–2020the periods are 70 years apart centre to centre, not 100

This case study compares 1926–1955, which starts a century ago, with 1996–2025, the latest 30 complete years. Both are 30 years long and they do not overlap. It uses tmax, as you did for Heathrow in Unit 4. Other choices are defensible (the normals 1901–1930 and 1991–2020, say, or tmin), and Part 2 uses all 173 years. Fix the choice now, before you see any results, so that it cannot be tuned to give the answer you like.

How this lesson works. Each step names its goal. The code cells start almost empty: you write the code, with # Load, # Inspect, # Clean, # Summarise and # Plot as your backbone. If you get stuck, press Hint; after your first run you can see a solution.

Working in Jupyter or Spyder instead? Download both files and save them in the same folder (right-click and choose Save link as… if your browser shows the text):

  • oxford-case-study.ipynb, a starter notebook with the headings for both parts
  • oxford.csv, the data it reads
Choose11a

Why compare two 30-year periods rather than the single years 1925 and 2025?

One year’s mean can sit a degree above or below its neighbours, so two single years mostly measure chance; 30 years average much of that out
Right: weather varies from year to year. A 30-year mean barely moves if you shift the period by a year; a single year’s mean can move a lot.
1925 has missing months, so it cannot be used
Every year in the file has 12 months of tmax; you will check that in the next step. The trouble with single years is noise, not gaps.
A 30-year comparison always shows a bigger change
A longer period does not push the difference up or down. It makes the answer depend less on which particular years you picked.
The Met Office publishes only 30-year averages
The file has every month since 1853. Thirty years is a convention for describing a climate, chosen because it smooths out year-to-year weather.

Your answer11a

sun has 912 gaps. Find the first year that has a sunshine value. How many whole years, from the start of the file, have no sunshine value at all?

That is the number of months. Each year has 12 of them.
Count both ends: the years 1853 to 1928 are 1928−1853+11928 - 1853 + 11928−1853+1 years.

Solution
  1. The first year with sunshine is 1929.
  2. The years before it are 1853 to 1928: 1929−1853=761929 - 1853 = 761929−1853=76 years.
  3. Check: 76×12=91276 \times 12 = 91276×12=912, so every sunshine gap is in those years.
The gaps have a story: sunshine was recorded at Oxford only from 1929. The one gap in tmin and af is a single month, December 1860; that is also why af, a count of days, has the dtype float64: NaN is a float.

What the inspection tells you: 2076 rows, 12 in each of 173 years; tmax has no gaps and runs from −0.2 °C (February 1947, a famously cold month) to 27.4 °C (July 2018). Both are plausible for Oxford, so there are no impossible values to deal with.

Clean for the question

★ essentialSP

Goal: decide what the question needs, keep that, and say why. Decide before you drop anything: every row you drop costs data (Lesson 7.4).

Choose11a

Why not run df.dropna() on the whole table?

It would drop every month before 1929, because sun is missing there, including 1926–1928 of the early period, although their tmax is fine
Right: dropna() drops a row with a gap in any column. It keeps 1164 of the 2076 rows, all from 1929 on, for a gap in a column the question does not use.
It would drop only December 1860, the month with no tmin
That forgets sun: 912 months have no sunshine value, and dropna() drops each of them too.
dropna() fills the gaps with 0, which would lower the mean
dropna() removes rows; it never fills. Filling is fillna().
No reason: fewer rows make the mean more accurate
Fewer rows make a mean less precise, since the SE is s/ns/\sqrt{n}s/n​. And here the rows lost are the old ones the question is about.

Your answer11a

By how many °C does the mean tmax of 1996–2025 exceed that of 1926–1955? Give two decimals.

That is early minus recent. “How much warmer is the recent period” is recent − early.
That is 2025 minus 1925, two single years: exactly the noisy comparison the 30-year periods avoid.
That is 1925–1954 against 1996–2025: the early period shifted by a year. Use .loc[1926:1955].

Print diff from your cell, or recent - early.

Every year has 12 months, so the mean of the 30 annual means equals the mean of all 360 monthly values. That is why you checked the months per year: with gaps, the two would differ.
